Microtubule-organizing centers (MTOCs) in oocytes

Number, composition and formation of MTOCs during oocyte growth

Microtubule-organizing centers (MTOCs) in mouse oocytes have been studied for a long time, but their basic features remained disputed until the recent study by Gu et al. (2025) (1). Even the number of MTOCs was an open question (2, 3). Gu et al. (2025) carried out a thorough investigation and found that MTOC number differs between mouse strains: C57BL/6J oocytes, like human oocytes (4), contain one MTOC, whereas CD1 (ICR) oocytes contain three (1). New MTOCs can form during meiotic maturation (5), but whether the original MTOC is required for later meiotic maturation or for embryonic development is unknown.

At least dozens of proteins have been identified in the MTOCs of mouse and human oocytes (1, 6), but how these proteins are arranged is still unknown. Centrioles have been identified in oocytes of primordial follicles (7, 8), whereas centrioles have not been reported in the MTOCs of oocytes in later, growing follicles (9, 10). How is this transition achieved?

In GV oocytes at the SN stage, the MTOC often has a bipartite shape (2). Why do CD1 oocytes have three MTOCs, whereas C57BL/6J oocytes have only one? I could understand two, but why three?

During oocyte growth, the deposition of proteins onto the MTOC may increase its ability to nucleate microtubules. However, at the later SN stage, the microtubule-organizing ability of the MTOC seems to decline compared with earlier stages (1). How does this happen?

The de novo formation of MTOCs during meiotic maturation (5) suggests that MTOC proteins are already present in the cytoplasm, but in small amounts (3, 11). How is this achieved, and when do these MTOCs form?

In mouse embryos, centrioles form de novo at the blastocyst stage (12), whereas in human embryos, centrioles are contributed by the sperm (13, 14). Why do the two species differ?

Many more questions remain to be answered.
